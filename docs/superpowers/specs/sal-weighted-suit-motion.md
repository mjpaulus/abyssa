# Sal in a weighted suit: how a Mark V diver actually moves

Michael, 2026-10-01: "his swimming and walking still dont seem like a person in a weighted
suit would move". Every earlier pass tuned Sal toward dry-land human gait norms (cadence ~110/min,
upright torso, straight stance leg) and the off-bottom mode toward a frogman's kick. This note
collects what the reference says, then turns it into numbers for three regimes: DECK (in air),
SEABED (in water, weighted) and OFF-BOTTOM (in the column).

## The rig, by weight

- US Navy Mark V standard rig: helmet and breastplate about 54-55 lb (25 kg), weight belt
  83-84 lb (38 kg), each boot 17.5 lb (7.9 kg), dress about 12 lb. Total about 190-200 lb (86-90 kg).
  [Wikipedia, Standard diving dress; Divers Institute of Technology; US Naval Undersea Museum]
- Mixed-gas Mark V was about 300 lb, 100 lb more. [US Naval Undersea Museum]
- In the film *Men of Honor* the reinstatement test is to walk **12 steps** in a 290 lb rig.
  It is staged as an ordeal. [Wikipedia, Men of Honor]
- Out of the water a fallen diver needs "at least four people to help raise him or her up",
  and two or three tenders dress him. [X-Ray Mag, "Heavy Metal"]

## What the reference says about movement

### On deck, in air
- The diver does not walk the deck in a finished rig. The 1943 Navy manual dresses him at the
  ladder: he "climbs over the side and stands on the diving ladder, with his waist at the
  level of the gunwale on which rests the weighted belt". The belt goes on there, then the
  helmet. [Diving Manual 1943, ch. X/XI]
- Modern dive-a-Mark-V outfits describe the walk to the ladder as slow, assisted and felt as
  weight on the shoulders and pull from the lines. [diveamarkv.com; search summary]
- Load-carriage biomechanics, the nearest measurable proxy for 40%+ body weight carried
  high: double support rises from about 29% to 38% of the step, step-width variability rises
  about 50%, and the trunk leans further forward as the load grows. Cadence and speed fall.
  [Huang & Kuo 2014, PMC3922835; backpack-load reviews, PMC9180465]
- **Observation for Sal:** about 90 kg hangs off his shoulders (helmet and corselet) and his
  hips (belt), with 16 kg on his feet. In air that gives a stiff, short, wide-based plod. He
  stays in double support for a long time, and each boot is lifted only just clear, swung
  through and set down flat. The trunk is pitched forward over the load. The lateral weight
  transfer is a whole-body shift over the stance boot, a waddle, not a tipping roll. The arms
  stand off the body because the corselet flares under them, and they barely swing.

### On the seabed, in water
- "The diver tends to lean forward against the drag of the water when walking on the bottom,
  and could often not see where they were putting their feet, so the toes are capped."
  [Wikipedia, Standard diving dress]
- "Most work using standard dress was done heavy, with the diver sufficiently negatively
  buoyant to walk on the bottom." [Wikipedia]
- On reaching the bottom the diver "adjusts his buoyancy to such a degree that the helmet
  merely lifts the weight of the apparatus off his shoulders". In water he is nearly neutral
  but kept negative. [Diving Manual 1943, ch. XI]
- "A diver can move with ease in slack water, but as the tide or current increases, it
  becomes increasingly difficult to advance. This difficulty may be lessened by advancing in a
  stooping or crawling position". Stooping lets air run into the dress and lightens him, so he
  has to vent with the chin valve. [Diving Manual 1943, ch. XIII]
- "To heavy and it was hard to walk. To light it was almost impossible to walk. There needs to
  be a half way point for easy solid walking." [ScubaBoard, hard-hat divers]
- Fully submerged walking (aquatic treadmill, neck depth): **0.45 m/s at about 68 steps/min**,
  against 74 at waist depth. Stride lengthens and cadence falls as buoyancy rises. People push
  against the water with each step and stay longer in single stance. With more buoyancy the
  heel strike disappears and they walk on their toes. [Jung et al. 2018, Sports Biomechanics]
- He carries a turn of hose on his arm "in order that a sudden pull from the surface would
  not cause him to lose his balance". [Diving Manual 1943]
- In a stumble he does not catch himself on a step. He opens the air ("check his descent by
  increasing his air supply valve") and the line is held. [Diving Manual 1943]
- **Observation for Sal:** on the seabed the walk is a forward-leaning slog in slow motion. The
  body is pitched into the water and the boots are dug in behind. Each step is a deliberate
  lift-swing-plant at a slow cadence. Between steps the body surges and then stalls against
  the drag, so it lurches rather than glides. The arms are held a little forward and out for
  balance and move slowly, as limbs moving through water do.

### Off the bottom, in the column
- "The bulkiness of fit, weighted boots and lack of fins made swimming impracticable."
  [Wikipedia]. From a working diver: "It is also possible to swim in it. Not to good but
  doable." [ScubaBoard]
- "Head-up vertical posture was the normal position". Pitching over sends air into the legs
  and toward blow-up, so the dress is laced at the legs to stop that. [Wikipedia; Diving
  Manual 1943]
- Up and down is done on the descending line with the legs locked around it, the right hand
  on the line and the left hand on the air valve. Ascent is made by inflating the dress, "he
  must be extremely careful as overinflation may result in his being 'blown' to the surface".
  [Diving Manual 1943, ch. XI and XIV]
- Getting off the bottom and settling is a buoyancy verb, and Space and C stay that verb.
  [Wikipedia; Naval Undersea Museum: "pressing a button to operate this valve... adjust his
  buoyancy"]
- **Observation for Sal:** there is no swimming stroke. Off the bottom he hangs upright and
  feet-down from an air-filled helmet, with the boots dangling heavy and slightly bent. He
  gets along by slow, wide, two-handed sweeps (reach forward and out, pull round to the hips,
  recover with the elbows bent) and by being drawn along. His legs answer with a small, slow
  wade, not a kick. At speed the body tilts into the travel the way a towed weight does (the
  boots swing back), but it never goes horizontal.

### The helmet does not turn
- "When you turn your head the helmet does not turn. Instead, you end up looking out a small
  side window." [X-Ray Mag]
- The bonnet is locked onto the breastplate, so to look round, a Mark V diver turns his
  shoulders and to look up he leans back. **For Sal, look and turn go into the torso and very
  little into the neck.**

### Stylised reference
- BioShock's Big Daddy suits are built on atmospheric and standard diving dress
  [Wikipedia, Big Daddy (BioShock)]. As a game read, a heavy suit is sold by slow cadence,
  long double support, arms held off the body, a forward-pitched trunk, and footfalls that
  land with a stop rather than roll through. A deliberate, low vertical bob reads heavier than
  a bouncy one.

## Targets

All lengths are in game units. Sal's leg (hip to sole) L = 1.50.

| | DECK (air) | SEABED (water) | OFF-BOTTOM | was (all) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| top speed | 1.5 u/s | 2.15 u/s | cruise about 15 u/s, sprint at least 23 u/s (shark escape) | 2.6 / 2.6 / 17.6 |
| cadence | 75-82 /min | 85-92 /min | stroke 0.35-0.42 Hz | 107-109 /min, kick 0.5 Hz |
| step / L | 0.75-0.80 | 0.90-1.00 | n/a | 0.97 |
| double support (time) | 0.36-0.42 | 0.30-0.36 | n/a | 0.19-0.21 |
| trunk lean, hips to neck | 7-10 deg | 11-15 deg | about 0 hanging, 12-20 deg at cruise | 1-1.5 deg |
| lateral | whole-body shift (hip sway 0.10-0.13), helmet sway at most 0.05, helmet roll at most 2 deg | hip sway at most 0.10 | n/a | hip 0.10, helmet 0.024 |
| helmet vertical bob | 0.04-0.06 | 0.05-0.08 | n/a | 0.10-0.12 |
| speed lurch per step (p2p / mean) | 0.15-0.30 | 0.20-0.40 | n/a | 0.04 |
| foot lift (sole centre, max) | 0.18-0.25 | 0.28-0.38 | boots hang, knee 10-25 deg | 0.45 |
| arms | out 15-22 deg, swing p2p 8-15 deg | forward/out, swing p2p 10-18 deg | two-hand sweep, arm p2p 60-90 deg | out 6 deg, swing 37 deg |
| legs off bottom | | | knee p2p at most 35 deg, mean 10-25 | knee p2p 83, mean 45 |
| ramp to 90% | 0.6-0.8 s | 0.8-1.2 s | 1.3-2.0 s | 0.3 / 0.6 / 0.8 s |
| look and turn | torso carries it, helmet at most 25% of the look | same | same | helmet about 80% |

These are taste-checked as well as sourced. The aquatic-treadmill speed (0.45 m/s) is about
4x slower, leg for leg, than any speed the game can live with, so the seabed keeps a
playable speed. It buys the weight with cadence, double support, lean and lurch instead.
